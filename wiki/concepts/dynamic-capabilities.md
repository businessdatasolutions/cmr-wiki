# Dynamic capabilities

**Dynamic capabilities** are a firm's higher-order capacity to *create, extend, and modify* its resource base in response to (and in anticipation of) environmental change. They are distinguishable from a firm's **operational capabilities** — the ordinary capacity to perform present-day tasks — and they govern the rate at which operational capabilities are themselves reshaped (Teece, 2007; Helfat & Winter, 2011).

Teece (2007) decomposes dynamic capabilities into three clusters, which the wiki uses as the structural backbone of [[warner-wager-process-model]]'s 15-cell vocabulary:

1. **Sensing** — scanning, creation, learning, and interpretative activity that analyses diverse information about trends in the business ecosystem. Identifying opportunities and threats.
2. **Seizing** — addressing opportunities through investment, decision making, and the orchestration of resources. Acting on what was sensed before the window closes.
3. **Transforming** — continuously renewing the firm's assets and structures to maintain evolutionary fitness. Making change durable at organisational scale.

Teece & Leih (2016) capture the distinction in a memorable phrase: *"ordinary capabilities are about doing things right; dynamic capabilities are about doing the right things."*

## Why the framework matters for digital-transformation work

The framework is the dominant lens in the strategic-management literature for explaining how firms respond to rapid technological and market change (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Helfat et al., 2007; Teece, 2007; Schilke et al., 2018). For digital-transformation work specifically, it is powerful because:

- **It separates the present from the renewable.** Operational capabilities can be outsourced to the cloud, replicated by competitors, or commoditised. Dynamic capabilities are harder to replicate and support evolutionary fitness — they are the durable source of competitive advantage as the ground keeps shifting.
- **It is microfoundational.** Each cluster (sensing / seizing / transforming) is itself composed of more concrete *microfoundations* — the activities, processes, and routines through which the capability is actually built. This makes the framework analytically tractable: instead of asking "does this firm have dynamic capabilities?" you can ask "which microfoundations are present, which are weak, which are missing?"
- **It links strategy → capabilities → business model.** DaSilva & Trkman (2014): "strategy entails devising dynamic capabilities able to respond to contingencies through the organization's business model. Business models are then bounded by the firm's dynamic capabilities." Dynamic capabilities are the intermediary between strategy and business model.

## Specialisation: digital dynamic capabilities

Warner & Wäger (2019) argue that the ubiquity of new digital technologies is changing the very nature and purpose of dynamic capabilities. Their two main claims:

1. **New scale economics.** New digital technologies (blockchain, cloud, IoT) change the nature of dynamic capabilities because organisations can now scale up or scale down their operations at a speed, ease, and cost that was not possible a decade ago.
2. **New scope.** The convergence and generativity of pervasive digital technologies means that the purpose of building dynamic capabilities is now paramount for a wider range of organisations — not just innovation-driven tech firms but traditional incumbents in mature industries.

The [[warner-wager-process-model]] is the operational specialisation: 9 digitally grounded microfoundations across sensing / seizing / transforming, plus a closed vocabulary of contextual contingencies (triggers / enablers / barriers) and three strategic-renewal outcomes (business model / collaborative approach / culture).

## Distinction from operational capabilities

| Aspect | Operational capabilities | Dynamic capabilities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | Maintain the status quo; deliver current outputs reliably | Renew the resource base; alter how the firm makes a living |
| **Time horizon** | Present | Medium / long term |
| **Source of advantage** | Efficiency of execution | Capacity for change |
| **Replicability** | Easily replicable; outsourceable | Hard to replicate; firm-specific |
| **Examples** | Accounting, HR ops, sales execution | Sensing trends, agile responses, ecosystem orchestration |

(Synthesised from Helfat & Winter, 2011; Teece, 2014, 2018.)

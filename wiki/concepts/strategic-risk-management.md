# Strategic risk management

**Strategic risk management** (SRM) is the discipline of treating risk as a *strategic* variable — not merely an operational threat to hedge or insure, but a managed input to the firm's decisions about where to compete, how aggressively to invest, and which exposures to take as a source of competitive advantage. The defining move that separates SRM from traditional risk management is the **upside reframe**: risk is treated as a mix of upside and downside, and the discipline becomes about *balancing* the two rather than minimising the latter.

The wiki's working definition follows Damodaran (2008):

> Good risk management is not about seeking out or avoiding risk, but about maintaining the right balance between the two.

## How SRM differs from traditional risk management

| Aspect | Traditional risk management | Strategic risk management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Default stance** | Minimise risk; hedge / insure / avoid | Allocate risk: avoid / pass-through / exploit |
| **Locus of ownership** | Finance / treasury function | Cross-functional; CEO-level concern |
| **Risk metric** | Variance reduction; loss avoidance | Value impact (firm valuation) |
| **Treatment of upside** | Mostly ignored | Central — exploited where firm has advantage |
| **Time horizon** | Short / discrete events | Continuous; embedded in strategic planning |
| **Organisational embedding** | Specialist function | Structural and cultural design choice |
| **Tools** | VaR, hedging instruments, insurance | All of the above + real options, decision trees, scenario analysis, simulation |

(Synthesised from Damodaran, 2008, ch 11 + ch 12.)

## Core operational moves

Three taxonomies anchor SRM in practice. All three come from Damodaran's framing; future sources may add or refine them.

### 1. Avoid / hedge / exploit allocation

For every risk a firm faces, classify into one of three buckets:

- **Exploit** — when the firm has an information, response-speed, flexibility, or resource advantage over competitors. Source of competitive advantage.
- **Hedge / insure** — when the firm can absorb the hedging cost cheaper than its investors can diversify it away. Classic worked example: Boeing's contract-specific FX exposure.
- **Pass through to investors** — when investors can diversify the risk away at lower cost than the firm can hedge. Most firm-specific risk for publicly traded firms.

The discipline is not in classifying any single risk, but in maintaining the *portfolio* of classifications consistently.

### 2. Five-axis risk taxonomy

Categorise each risk along five axes (which combine orthogonally) so that the management response can be matched to the risk's shape:

- Diversifiable vs non-diversifiable
- Small vs large (size is relative to the firm)
- Symmetric vs asymmetric (in terms of upside-vs-downside payoff distribution)
- Short-term vs long-term
- Continuous (e.g. FX, rates) vs discontinuous (e.g. terrorism, natural disaster)

Plus a *perspective* axis (line-manager / top-manager / investor) — pick one and stay consistent.

### 3. Five ingredients of the successful risk-taking organisation

The structural design conditions under which SRM works (per Damodaran ch 12, drawing on ch 11):

1. **Alignment of interests** — compensation, governance, ownership structures so decision-makers behave like owners.
2. **Good and timely information** — judged by crisis-mode performance, not steady-state.
3. **Solid analysis** — decision trees, simulations, scenario analysis, VaR; tool understanding more critical than tool access.
4. **Flexibility** — adapt faster than competitors; correlated with flat structure, smaller size, less vested in existing tech.
5. **People** — seek out and retain people who respond well to risk.

## Why SRM matters

Three convergent shifts in the operating environment make SRM increasingly load-bearing for most firms:

1. **Risk globalisation.** Interconnected markets mean local shocks propagate; a 9% Shanghai drop dragged the Dow down 4.3% the next morning (Feb 27, 2007).
2. **Cross-sector spillover.** Risks cross business boundaries — sub-prime mortgage laxity in 2007 hit *Yahoo* through ad-revenue dependence on lenders.
3. **Financial-market risk emergence.** Firms with healthy operations are increasingly exposed to financial-market turbulence they don't control.

These shifts erode the assumption that risk is *containable* within functional silos. SRM is the organisational answer.

## Boundaries — what SRM is not

- It is **not** risk hedging (per Principle 7). Hedging is one tool among many; the strategic discipline is about which risks to hedge and which not to.
- It is **not** a finance function (per Principle 9). Even though many SRM tools are finance-derived, the discipline cuts across functional areas.
- It is **not** about eliminating risk. Per Principle 2, a firm that focuses on reducing earnings or stock-price volatility will end up *mismanaging* risk — too little exploited, too much hedged.

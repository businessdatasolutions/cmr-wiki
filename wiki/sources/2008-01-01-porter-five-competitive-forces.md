# The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy — Porter (2008)

> Awareness of the five forces can help a company understand the structure of its industry and stake out a position that is more profitable and less vulnerable to attack.

(HBR's framing of the article — Porter's 2008 update of his revolutionary 1979 HBR article *"How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy"*.)

## TL;DR

Porter's **Five Forces** framework holds that an industry's long-run profitability is determined not by today's direct rivals alone but by the **collective strength of five competitive forces**: (1) threat of new entrants, (2) bargaining power of suppliers, (3) bargaining power of buyers, (4) threat of substitutes, and (5) rivalry among existing competitors. The strongest force(s) govern profitability and should anchor strategy. The 2008 update reaffirms the 1979 original, corrects common misreadings (industry growth, technology, government, and complements are *not* a "sixth force" — they act *through* the five), and gives practical guidance: position where the forces are weakest, exploit shifts in the forces, and reshape the forces in your favour.

This is the canonical primary source for the [[porter-five-forces]] concept page.

## Context (WHY)

Porter's thesis is that **the job of the strategist is to understand and cope with competition** — but managers habitually define competition too narrowly, as rivalry among today's direct competitors. Profit-eroding competition is broader: it includes customers, suppliers, potential entrants, and substitutes. "Extended rivalry" from all five forces defines an industry's structure. The underlying drivers of profitability are the *same* across superficially different industries (autos, art masterpieces, European health-care delivery) — analyse the five forces and the structure becomes legible.

The framing matters strategically because **industry structure — not whether an industry is high-tech or low-tech, emerging or mature, regulated or not — drives profitability** in the medium-to-long run. A healthy structure should be as much a strategist's concern as the firm's own position.

## Methods (HOW)

Conceptual framework, not an empirical study. Porter draws on the industrial-organization economics tradition and decades of strategy research, illustrated through a large set of named industry examples (commercial aviation, heavy trucks/Paccar, microprocessors/Intel, soft drinks, photographic film/Kodak–Fuji, ERP/SAP, music distribution/Apple iTunes, airlines, casinos). The one quantitative exhibit (industry ROIC 1992–2006) uses Standard & Poor's Compustat data with author's calculations.

## Results (WHAT) — the framework

### The five forces

1. **Threat of new entrants.** New entrants bring capacity and a hunger for share, capping profit potential. The threat depends on **entry barriers** and **expected retaliation**. Porter names *seven sources of entry barriers*: (1) supply-side economies of scale, (2) demand-side benefits of scale (network effects), (3) customer switching costs, (4) capital requirements, (5) incumbency advantages independent of size (proprietary tech, locations, brand, experience), (6) unequal access to distribution channels, (7) restrictive government policy. It is the *threat* of entry, not actual entry, that holds down profitability.
2. **Bargaining power of suppliers.** Powerful suppliers capture value via higher prices or shifted costs. A supplier group is powerful if it is more concentrated than the industry it sells to, doesn't depend heavily on the industry, imposes switching costs, offers differentiated products, has no substitute, or can credibly threaten forward integration. (Microsoft eroding PC-maker profits via OS pricing.)
3. **Bargaining power of buyers.** Powerful customers force down prices and demand more. Buyers have leverage when few/large, products are standardized, switching costs are low, or they can threaten backward integration; they are *price sensitive* when the purchase is a large fraction of cost, they earn low profits, or product quality matters little to their own output.
4. **Threat of substitutes.** A substitute performs the same/similar function by a different means (videoconferencing↔travel, plastic↔aluminium, email↔express mail). Substitutes cap prices. The threat is high when the substitute offers an attractive price-performance trade-off and switching costs are low. Substitutes are easy to overlook because they may look very different from the industry's product.
5. **Rivalry among existing competitors.** Intensity rises with numerous/equal competitors, slow industry growth, high exit barriers, and high commitment. Rivalry is *most destructive* when it gravitates to price (transfers profit straight to customers) — likeliest when products are near-identical, fixed costs are high / marginal costs low, capacity must be added in large increments, or the product is perishable. Non-price rivalry (features, service, brand) is less destructive and can be positive-sum when rivals serve different segments.

### Factors that are NOT a sixth force

Porter is explicit that **industry growth rate, technology & innovation, government, and complementary products are not separate forces**. They influence profitability only *through* their effect on the five forces. A common strategic error is mistaking these visible attributes for the underlying structure (e.g. assuming fast-growing or high-tech industries are automatically attractive — personal computers were fast-growing yet among the least profitable).

### Implications for strategy

Three moves: **(a) position** the company where the forces are weakest (Paccar's focus on owner-operator truckers → 68 straight profitable years, >20% long-run ROE, 10% price premium); **(b) exploit shifts** in the forces (Apple stepping into the music-distribution vacuum with iTunes); **(c) reshape** the balance of forces in the firm's favour (standardise inputs to neutralise supplier power; expand services to counter buyer power; raise fixed costs of competing to deter entrants).

## Dynamic-capabilities reading

Tagged `contextual/external-triggers`. The Five Forces is the classic tool for reading the **external competitive triggers** a firm faces — Porter explicitly analyses how *disruptive substitutes* and *new entrants* (the internet reshaping music distribution, travel agents, long-distance telephony) shift industry structure, which is exactly the `external-triggers` cell of the W&W process model (disruptive competitors / changing consumer behaviours / disruptive digital technologies). The framework predates and underspecifies the *digital* dimension, but it is the analytical ancestor of digital-sensing work. Role-relevance inherits the cell defaults (`ceo, cso, cdo, cmo, strategy-consultant`).

## Discussion / Significance (SO WHAT)

For this wiki the Five Forces is foundational scaffolding for the strategy cluster:

1. **It defines the external/industry layer of strategy** that complements the internal-capability layer ([[dynamic-capabilities]], [[mckinsey-7s-framework]]) and the risk layer ([[strategic-risk-management]]).
2. **It connects to the risk lens.** Industry structure is itself a strategic risk surface — a firm's exposure to entrant, substitute, and buyer-power shocks is a function of the five forces. Damodaran's "exploit / hedge / pass-through" allocation operates *within* a structure the five forces describe.
3. **It anchors substitute/disruption analysis** that the Strategyzer business-model-innovation sources ([[2020-03-16-explore-exploit-continuum]], [[2019-05-19-business-model-evolution-portfolio-map]]) treat from the model-design side.

**Limitations (acknowledged):** the framework is a static, structure-centric snapshot; critics note it underweights dynamics, complementors, and capability-based competition (addressed by the resource-based view and dynamic-capabilities literature already in this wiki). Porter's 2008 rebuttal — that growth, tech, government, complements act *through* the forces — is itself contested by ecosystem/platform theorists.

## Debates and supersession

_Single-source page._ The dynamic-capabilities and resource-based-view literatures ([[dynamic-capabilities]], [[warner-wager-process-model]]) offer a *capability-side* complement (and partial critique) to Porter's structure-side view; this is a productive-tension to develop in a future synthesis, not a supersession.

# Strategy Maps — Soundview Executive Book Summary (2004)

> More than 75 percent of the average company's market value comes from intangible assets that traditional metrics don't measure. The Balanced Scorecard is a revolutionary performance measurement system that allows organizations to quantify critical intangible assets, such as people, information and culture. Now the people who first developed the Balanced Scorecard, Robert S. Kaplan and David P. Norton, have created a powerful new tool based on their ongoing research. The strategy map allows companies to describe the links between intangible assets and value creation so all aspects of strategy can be implemented in a manner that ensures sustained value creation.

(Soundview's "The Summary in Brief" — i.e. *their* framing, not Kaplan & Norton's verbatim words.)

## Context (WHY)

Kaplan & Norton developed the Balanced Scorecard (BSC) in the early 1990s as a performance-measurement system that supplements financial measures with three additional perspectives (customer / internal / learning & growth). *Strategy Maps* (2004) is the third book in their BSC trilogy and adds the **strategy map** — a visual framework that makes the *cause-and-effect relationships* between BSC perspectives explicit. The argument: organisations can describe strategy clearly, align resources to it, and implement it consistently when they have a one-page picture of how intangible assets → internal processes → customer value → financial outcomes connect.

The summary's framing emphasises **intangible assets** (human, information, organisation capital) as the source of 75%+ of company market value and the need for a measurement system that can quantify their alignment with strategy.

## Methods (HOW)

K&N's primary methodology (as condensed in the summary): the strategy map is a **conceptual framework synthesised from BSC practice with their consulting clients**, anchored by named worked cases. The summary mentions Amanco (Latin American plastic pipes; triple-bottom-line strategy map) and IBM (complete-customer-solution strategy 1960–1980) as illustrative cases. Empirical validation is not foregrounded in the summary; this is a framework-and-cases book, not a quantitative-evidence book.

## Results (WHAT) — the strategy map's structure

The summary reproduces the canonical strategy-map diagram on its first content page. Four **perspectives**, stacked vertically, with cause-and-effect arrows running upward:

1. **Financial perspective (top)** — *Long-term shareholder value*. Two sub-strategies meet here: **Productivity Strategy** (Improve cost structure + Increase asset utilization) and **Growth Strategy** (Expand revenue opportunities + Enhance customer value).
2. **Customer perspective** — *Customer Value Proposition*, decomposed into Product/Services Attributes (Price / Quality / Availability / Selection / Functionality), Relationship (Service / Partnership), and Image (Brand).
3. **Internal perspective** — Four families of value-creating processes:
   - **Operations Management Processes** — Supply / Production / Distribution / Risk Management.
   - **Customer Management Processes** — Selection / Acquisition / Retention / Growth.
   - **Innovation Processes** — Opportunity ID / R&D Portfolio / Design–Develop / Launch.
   - **Regulatory & Social Processes** — Environment / Safety & Health / Employment / Community.
4. **Learning and Growth perspective (bottom)** — Three components of **intangible assets**:
   - **Human Capital** — strategic competencies. Four steps to align with strategy: identify strategic job families → build competency profile → assess human capital readiness → human capital development program.
   - **Information Capital** — technology infrastructure + information capital applications. 5–15% of IT budget for strategic-alignment investment (vs the typical 90% to maintaining existing applications).
   - **Organization Capital** — Culture / Leadership / Alignment / Teamwork.

The cause-and-effect chain: aligned intangible assets → critical few internal processes → customer value proposition → financial outcomes (productivity + growth) → long-term shareholder value.

### Risk management as a strategic process

The summary names risk management as one of four value-creating-process families under **Operations Management**, citing Microsoft (temporary-worker policy) and Disney (climate-stable theme-park siting) as illustrations. The treatment is light — risk management is one bullet among many — but worth noting because it sits at the intersection with the wiki's risk-management cluster (Damodaran 2008 chapters already ingested).

The summary's risk-management framing is much more *operational* than Damodaran's: "modify operations to manage risk" rather than "allocate risk into avoid/hedge/exploit buckets". The two framings do not directly contradict but operate at different levels. **Future synthesis opportunity** when the primary book lands.

### Three customer-value-proposition archetypes

The summary lists four strategic-positioning archetypes (with the customer-management-process emphasis varying):

- **Low total cost strategy** — focus on Operations Management processes.
- **Product leadership strategy** — focus on Innovation processes.
- **Complete customer solution strategy** — focus on Customer Management processes. (IBM 1960–1980 as canonical case.)
- **Lock-in strategy** — high switching costs; industry standards; razor-and-blade dependencies. (Many internal-process families.)

### Six-step campaign-planning process

The summary's procedural recipe for actually building a strategy map:

1. Define the shareholder/stakeholder value gap.
2. Reconcile the customer value proposition.
3. Establish the value time line.
4. Identify strategic themes.
5. Create strategic asset readiness.
6. Identify and fund the strategic initiatives.

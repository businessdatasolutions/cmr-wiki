# McKinsey 7S Model — CFI explainer (Vipond 2020)

> A tool that analyzes a company's "organizational design." The goal of the model is to depict how effectiveness can be achieved in an organization through the interactions of seven key elements — Structure, Strategy, Skill, System, Shared Values, Style, and Staff.

(CFI's framing of the model.)

## TL;DR

**This is a secondary explainer.** The wiki holds a ~4-minute Corporate Finance Institute (CFI) article by Tim Vipond (FMVA, 7 July 2020) describing the **McKinsey 7S Model** — a framework (originally developed at McKinsey by Tom Peters, Robert Waterman, Richard Pascale and Anthony Athos around 1980) for analysing organisational design via seven interdependent elements. The seven split into **three "Hard Ss"** (Structure, Strategy, Systems) and **four "Soft Ss"** (Shared Values, Skills, Style, Staff), with **Shared Values at the centre**. The model's central claim is *interconnectedness*: change one element and a domino effect ripples through the others, so effective change requires re-aligning all seven.

## Results (WHAT) — the seven elements

**Hard Ss** (tangible, management-directed):

1. **Structure** — the chain of command and accountability; the org chart.
2. **Strategy** — the business plan for sustainable competitive advantage, reinforced by mission and values.
3. **Systems** — the business and technical infrastructure: workflows and decision-making chains.

**Soft Ss** (intangible, culture-driven):

4. **Skills** — the capabilities and competencies that let employees achieve objectives.
5. **Style** — the management/leadership style; the code of conduct set by senior staff's interactions and symbolic decisions.
6. **Staff** — talent management: recruiting, training, rewards.
7. **Shared Values** (centre) — mission, objectives and values; the foundation that aligns all other elements. Founder values changing here ripple everywhere.

### Application (top-down, four steps)

1. Identify areas not effectively aligned (gaps/inconsistencies among values, strategy, structure, systems).
2. Determine the optimal organisational design (no off-the-shelf template — requires research).
3. Decide where and what changes to make (hierarchy, communication flow, reporting).
4. Make the necessary changes (implementation is make-or-break; needs a plan).

CFI's worked example: a **merger** disrupts Structure, Staff, and Strategy simultaneously; 7S is used to find the inconsistent areas and re-organise.

### Advantages / disadvantages (per CFI)

- **Advantages:** coherent/"synced" action across the company; tracks the impact of changes across elements; long-standing and widely adopted.
- **Disadvantages:** long-term in nature; uncertain how it adapts to fast-changing businesses; **relies on internal factors** — weak where external circumstances dominate.

## Discussion / Significance (SO WHAT)

1. **It is the internal-alignment counterpart to Porter's external-structure view.** Where [[porter-five-forces]] analyses the industry, 7S analyses the firm's internal coherence — together they bracket the strategy-execution problem.
2. **It overlaps the Kaplan & Norton strategy-map logic** ([[2004-04-01-kaplan-norton-2004-strategy-maps-soundview-summary]]): both insist that strategy fails without alignment of the soft/intangible elements (skills, culture, leadership) to the hard elements (structure, systems, processes).
3. **It is a recognisable practitioner vocabulary** ("the seven Ss", "hard vs soft Ss") worth having as a discoverable concept page.

**Limitations:** secondary source; CFI omits the model's authorship and empirical grounding; the model is descriptive (no metric, no causal weighting between elements).

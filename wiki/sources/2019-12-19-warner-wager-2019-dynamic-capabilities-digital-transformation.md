# Building dynamic capabilities for digital transformation: An ongoing process of strategic renewal

> In this qualitative study, we explore how incumbent firms in traditional industries build dynamic capabilities for digital transformation. Digital transformation has been defined as the use of new digital technologies, such as mobile, artificial intelligence, cloud, blockchain, and the Internet of things (IoT) technologies, to enable major business improvements to augment customer experience, streamline operations, or create new business models. In making sense of digital transformation, we discovered that leaders in various industry circles use the term inconsistently to describe various strategizing and organizing activities; in addition, the term has gained limited scholarly attention as a context for study of strategic change. Drawing on senior executives' experiences with leading digitalization projects at incumbent firms, we propose a process model comprising of nine microfoundations to reveal the generic contingency factors that trigger, enable, and hinder the building of dynamic capabilities for digital transformation. Our findings reveal that digital transformation is an ongoing process of using new digital technologies in everyday organizational life, which recognizes agility as the core mechanism for the strategic renewal of an organization's (1) business model, (2) collaborative approach, and eventually the (3) culture.

## TL;DR

Warner & Wäger empirically ground a **process model of digital transformation** consisting of **nine microfoundations** organised across Teece's sensing / seizing / transforming triad, plus a **closed vocabulary of contextual contingencies** (3 external triggers, 3 internal enablers, 3 internal barriers). Their core conceptual move is to define digital transformation as the **ongoing strategic renewal** of three nested layers — business model → collaborative approach → culture — with agility as the central mechanism. The paper is the canonical reference for the wiki's `dynamic_capabilities:` tagging system; the 15-cell closed vocabulary lives on [[warner-wager-process-model]].

## Context (WHY)

Digital transformation has become a strategic imperative on senior-leadership agendas, but the literature gives little conceptual or empirical purchase on *how* incumbent firms actually build the capabilities to transform digitally. The authors locate three problems in the existing scholarship:

1. **Terminological drift.** Leaders use "digital transformation" inconsistently to describe a wide range of strategizing and organizing activities — from technology adoption to mindset shifts to business-model reinvention. This is corrosive for both practice and research.
2. **A capability-building gap.** The dynamic-capabilities framework (Teece, 2007; Helfat & Peteraf, 2015) provides a powerful lens on how firms respond to environmental change, but research at the *microfoundations* level — *what specifically* a firm does to build sensing, seizing, and transforming capabilities — was scant for the digital context.
3. **An incumbent-firm specificity.** Incumbents face the dual pressure of needing to balance exploitation of path-dependent existing capabilities with the building of new digital capabilities. Born-digital pioneers (Google, Amazon, Facebook) are not facing this trade-off. The relevant empirical question is the incumbent's, not the digital native's.

The paper anchors itself in Agarwal & Helfat's (2009) **strategic-renewal perspective**, which the authors use to redefine the *scope* of a digital transformation: not a one-shot technology adoption but an ongoing process of refreshing or replacing an organisation's (1) business model, (2) collaborative approach, and eventually (3) culture.

## Methods (HOW)

Multiple-case qualitative interpretive research over a 21-month window (Oct 2016 – Apr 2018), conducted with seven incumbent German MNCs across automotive, industrial-equipment, banking, telecoms, energy, automotive (a second case), and media-publishing industries (anonymised as Motion AG, Powerhouse AG, Balance AG, Voice AG, Energy AG, Drive AG, Media Inc.). The novel design feature is the addition of **six strategy-consultancy partners** as a parallel data source — the authors first interviewed senior consultants who had authored published industry reports on digitalisation, then returned to the field to interview senior executives at the incumbent firms themselves.

Data sources:

- 18 in-depth interviews (12 with consultancy partners + 11 with senior digital executives at the incumbents; some interviewees overlap across rounds).
- 17 strategy-consultancy reports on digital transformation (6–96 pp each, 2013–2016).
- 105 company news reports across the 7 case firms.
- 80 published consultancy reports.
- 10 weeks of participant observation at one of the consultancies (Digitize Ltd.).
- Annual reports of the case firms (2000–2017 where available).

Analytical strategy follows the Gioia methodology: summative content analysis of the consultancy reports first to surface the inconsistencies in how "digital transformation" is used, then in-case coding into first-order activities via NVivo 11, aggregation into second-order themes, and cross-case comparison to extract the aggregate dimensions (the 9 microfoundations + 3 contextual-factor groups). The data structure is presented in Fig. 1 and reproduced in tabular form in Appendix 1.

Honest scoping that the authors themselves flag: the model is grounded in incumbent-firm observations from one country (Germany), one regional anchor (large traditional industries), and a practitioner perspective. The transferability to new ventures, born-digital firms, and moderately dynamic environments is unclear. The authors call for survey research operationalising the framework as the natural next step.

## Results (WHAT)

The headline result is the **nine-microfoundation process model** (Fig. 2), organised across Teece's sensing/seizing/transforming triad:

**Digital sensing.** Three microfoundations:

1. **Digital scouting** — informal and formal scouting networks in technology hubs to identify trends; competitor-intelligence gathering; sensing customer-centric trends.
2. **Digital scenario planning** — analyzing scouted signals; interpreting digital future scenarios; formulating digital strategies (replaces the rigid 3–5-year strategic plan).
3. **Digital mindset crafting** — establishing a long-term digital vision; enabling an entrepreneurial mindset; promoting a digital mindset throughout the organisation.

The authors emphasise that traditional formal strategic planning has not kept up with the speed of digital disruption — sensing capabilities must explicitly incorporate the analytical affordances of AI, analytics, and IoT platforms to make sense of "big" or "smart" data.

**Digital seizing.** Three microfoundations:

1. **Strategic agility** — pacing strategic responses; rapidly reallocating resources; accepting redirection and change as a way of life.
2. **Rapid prototyping** — creating minimum viable products; lean-start-up methodology; digital innovation labs.
3. **Balancing digital portfolios** — balancing internal vs external options; scaling up innovative business models; setting an appropriate speed of execution.

Strategic agility is named as the *critical* dynamic capability for the digital age — the authors argue agility is the core mechanism through which strategic renewal happens.

**Digital transforming.** Three microfoundations:

1. **Navigating innovation ecosystems** — joining a digital ecosystem; interacting with multiple external partners; exploiting new ecosystem capabilities.
2. **Redesigning internal structures** — hiring a chief digital officer; digitalisation of business models (in the structural sense); designing team-based structures.
3. **Improving digital maturity** — identifying digital workforce maturity; external recruiting of digital talent; leveraging digital knowledge inside the firm.

**Contextual factors** — three groups of three:

- **External triggers**: disruptive digital competitors / changing consumer behaviours / disruptive digital technologies.
- **Internal enablers**: cross-functional teams / fast decision making / executive support.
- **Internal barriers**: rigid strategic planning / change resistances / high level of hierarchy.

**Three forms of strategic renewal** — the second contribution. Cross-case analysis (Table 3) shows that digital transformation does not always operate at the same scope. Three nested layers, in order of depth:

1. Renewal of the **business model** — refreshing or replacing the value proposition (e.g., Drive AG: ownership model → mobility-services + ownership).
2. Renewal of the **collaborative approach** — internal collaboration patterns, and external partnerships with the wider ecosystem (e.g., Voice AG: internal R&D → open R&D alliances).
3. Renewal of the organisational **culture** — the deepest and slowest layer (e.g., Motion AG, Media Inc.: "Dynamic Digital" campaign / cross-functional digital innovation division refreshing corporate culture wholesale).

The authors note that scope tends to follow this order — culture refreshment lags business-model and collaborative-approach renewal — which gives the model a maturity-curve flavour the paper does not over-claim.

**Conceptual definition (verbatim):**

> Digital transformation is an ongoing process of strategic renewal that uses advances in digital technologies to build capabilities that refresh or replace an organization's business model, collaborative approach, and culture. (p. 344)

## Discussion / Significance (SO WHAT)

For the wiki, this paper is **foundational**: CLAUDE.md's `dynamic_capabilities:` tagging system, the [`neighbour-source-scan`](../../.claude/skills/neighbour-source-scan) skill's Path A, and the entire "thematic adjacency index" architecture all rest on the 15-cell W&W vocabulary. The paper is the canonical citation for the closed vocabulary, and the [[warner-wager-process-model]] concept page is the wiki's operational interface to it.

Three downstream implications worth flagging:

1. **For CMR (customer/marketing/something equivalent) projects** — the paper's empirical claim that digital transformation tends to deepen from business-model → collaborative-approach → culture should hold as a maturity model: organisations that have refreshed their business model but not yet touched their collaborative approach are at an earlier stage than those that have. This is testable as a CMR-maturity diagnostic.
2. **For agility as a load-bearing concept** — the paper names strategic agility as the *central* mechanism. Other corpus sources that arrive will need to position themselves against this claim (do they agree agility is central? do they contradict?).
3. **For the seven case firms** — Motion AG, Powerhouse AG, Balance AG, Voice AG, Energy AG, Drive AG, Media Inc. are all anonymised. Their real identities are not in the paper. Treat them as pseudonymous case data, not as case studies of named firms; do not promote them to entity pages.

Limitations the authors acknowledge:

- Single country (Germany), single context (large traditional incumbents) — transferability to new ventures and to other markets is unclear.
- Practitioner-perspective qualitative methods — does not measure the long-term effects of digital transformation on growth or performance.
- Cross-case design with 7 firms over a 21-month window — limited time-depth on whether the renewal sequence (BM → collab → culture) actually plays out in firms tracked longitudinally.

Limitations the authors do *not* foreground:

- The senior-executive perspective is one-sided: middle managers and digital-native employees are not represented, even though the paper repeatedly cites their role in cross-functional teams and digital-mindset crafting.
- The consultancy-mediated lens (12 of the 18 interviews are with consultants who are not at the case firms themselves) builds in a structural bias toward management-consultancy framings of digital transformation. The paper is honest about its sample but does not engage with what this lens forecloses.

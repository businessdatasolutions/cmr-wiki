# The Power of Strategic Centering — McGrath (2026)

> In a dematerializing economy — where value has shifted from physical assets to intangibles such as data, software, and capabilities — traditional strategy frameworks no longer provide enough guidance. Organizations need a clear center: a single, coherent organizing principle that defines what they are really about.

(HBR's summary framing of the article.)

## TL;DR

Rita McGrath argues that the strategy frameworks executives inherited — Porter's [[porter-five-forces|Five Forces]], the resource-based view (VRIO), Blue Ocean — were "forged in the **stuff economy**," when ~83% of Fortune 500 assets (1975) were tangible. Today ~90% of corporate value is **intangible**, and McGrath calls the transition **dematerialization**. As industry boundaries dissolve, the old anchors (defensible position, stable industry, long-lived physical assets) lose their power. Her replacement is **strategic centering**: deliberately choosing *one organizing principle* to guide resource allocation, opportunity selection, and organizational identity — answering "**What are we really about?**" She identifies **five centers** (mission / customer / technology / national ecosystem / friction erasure), each with a core question, a best-fit condition, and a characteristic risk, and argues a center enables **permissionless action** (people act without waiting for approval because they share the direction).

## Context (WHY)

The article situates itself at a **turning point** in Carlota Perez's model of technological revolutions (we are mid-transition between the mass-production paradigm and the information/telecom paradigm). The symptoms McGrath names — inequality, instability, an exhausted-but-unreplaced strategic paradigm — frame *dematerialization* as a `contextual/external-triggers` shock that strands the inherited frameworks. This is the explicit point of contact with [[2008-01-01-porter-five-competitive-forces]]: McGrath names Five Forces, RBV and Blue Ocean as stuff-economy tools and argues they are "dangerously incomplete" for a dematerialized world.

## Argument (HOW)

**What centering is — and isn't.** Centering ≠ purpose (too aspirational to guide decisions), ≠ vision (a static destination, not a dynamic direction), ≠ core competence (a prison when the technology shifts). It is closer to Levitt's "what business are you in?" but goes further: choosing the *dimension* along which a firm pursues a coherent opportunity set as industries dissolve.

**A center does three things:** (1) **bounds the opportunity set without closing it** — you pursue possibilities that share a common logic; (2) **resolves capital-allocation dilemmas** — once the center is clear, resources flow toward it (the `digital-seizing/balancing-digital-portfolios` move); (3) **enables permissionless action** — shared understanding of the center lets people act without approval, increasing speed and reducing the return on internal politics.

## Results (WHAT)

The article is conceptual (no dataset). Its central empirical illustration is **Novartis under Vas Narasimhan**: centering on "innovative medicines in specific therapy areas" justified the early, risky radioligand-therapy acquisitions and the divestment of consumer health, eye care (Alcon) and generics (Sandoz). McGrath reports the separated companies are now worth ~2× Novartis's 2018 combined valuation (~$190–200B → Novartis ~$300B + Alcon ~$40B + Sandoz ~$35B + Haleon ~$40B), with Novartis shrinking from 126,000 to 76,000 employees — her evidence that a clear center beats conglomerate breadth.

**The five centers** (each illustrated): Mission (Novartis, Shopify), Customer (Amazon, Airbnb's Chesky "founder mode" recentering), Technology (Fujifilm vs Kodak, Shell "molecule company" vs BP "integrated energy", Nvidia), National ecosystem (TSMC/Taiwan "silicon shield", Samsung/Korea), Friction erasure (Toss, DBS, the platform plays of Google/Apple/Facebook). McGrath then **matches center type to how dematerialized an industry is** (physical-output → ecosystem/technology; partially dematerialized → mission/customer; digital-value-but-legacy-complexity → friction erasure; already-digital → mission/customer).

**Leadership finding (the surprise).** Leaders of centered organizations are *not* hands-off servant-leaders but **deeply engaged, highly visible** presences who "maintain the center" — sensing weak signals (Narasimhan and radioligands), giving direction without dictating method. This is the [[2019-12-19-warner-wager-2019-dynamic-capabilities-digital-transformation|sensing]] microfoundation in practitioner dress, and it is what makes permissionlessness produce speed rather than confusion.

## Discussion

Strategic centering reads as the latest move in McGrath's long argument against durable positioning (*The End of Competitive Advantage*, transient advantage): if advantage is transient and industries dissolve, the stable thing a firm can hold is not a *position* but a *center*. It is therefore a direct critique of [[porter-five-forces]] and the resource-based view, and a strong complement to [[dynamic-capabilities]] (centering is how a firm decides *what* to sense and seize) and [[organizational-ambidexterity]] (a center keeps the explore portfolio coherent rather than scattered). The "permissionless action" claim links to [[two-way-door-decisions]] and [[smart-simplicity]] (reducing the return on politics ≈ enabling cooperation over structure). Within this wiki it bears directly on [[cmr-framework]]: a "center" is precisely the kind of single organizing principle a firm's capability engine would be tuned to, and McGrath's "leaders sensing weak signals" maps onto the helix's **Sense** phase.
